A field experiment · one guitar

Tune up, or tune down?

Guitarists are told to always finish tuning a string upward. I spent five days measuring whether it’s still true.

Experiment & data by Egg Syntax · Data analysis & writing by Claude Opus 4.8

Instrument: PRS SE McCarty 594 Singlecut (2023) Window: 10–14 Aug 2026 Measurements: 138 Threshold: 3 cents

There’s a piece of advice that circulates among guitarists: when you tune a string, finish by bringing it up to the right pitch, not down. The idea is that easing up into pitch leaves the string seated and settled, while coming down from above leaves a little slack that later slips — so the string drifts out of tune sooner.

It’s plausible, it’s widely repeated, and it may well have been true for older strings and hardware. But is it still true today? I decided to actually measure it.

How the test worked

The trick to a fair test is separating the thing I care about — tuning direction — from two things that muddy it:

The weather. Temperature and humidity constantly nudge every string at once, so you can’t just tune up one day, down the next, and compare.

The strings themselves. A thick wound low E behaves differently from a thin plain high E no matter how you tune it.

So I did two things. On every check, half the strings were finished up and half finished down, alternating across the neck. Because all six are checked at the same moment, whatever the weather is doing hits both groups equally and cancels out. Then I ran the whole thing again with the roles swapped — the strings tuned up in the first run were tuned down in the second. If tuning up wins in both runs, it’s really about direction; if the same physical strings win both times, it’s just those strings being stubborn. Running it both ways tells those apart.

Over about five days I repeatedly checked all six strings and noted any that had drifted more than 3 cents off pitch. (A cent is a hundredth of a semitone; 3 cents is roughly the smallest error a good ear starts to notice.) That gave 23 full check-ins across the two runs, a total of 138 data points.

Strings finished by tuning up went out of tune noticeably less often — a gap of about 16 percentage points — and sat closer to pitch on average across all the checks. When strings were out of tune, the ones tuned down weren’t much further out than the ones tuned up. So the advantage of tuning up isn’t that it shrinks the misses — it’s that it makes them happen less often. That’s where the overall gap comes from.

How sure am I?

Fairly, for this guitar. The advantage showed up in both runs and for five of the six strings — and the two mild exceptions disagreed with each other, so no single string was quietly working against the pattern. Putting a number on it, the analysis puts the probability that tuning up genuinely helps at roughly 96–99%, for both how often and how far.

I also stress-tested the statistical analysis. Because a guitar’s strings share one neck and react to the same room together, the measurements aren’t fully independent, and naive statistics can be fooled by that. Redoing the analysis to account for it didn’t weaken the conclusion — if anything it held up slightly better, because that shared movement affects the up and down strings together and cancels out of the comparison.

Caveats

The big one: this is one guitar over five days. Enough to see a clear pattern on this instrument — not enough to declare a universal law. A different guitar, different strings, older hardware, or a different climate could shift the result. Treat it as a well-controlled case study, not the final word.

  • Strings were re-tuned only to within 3 cents, not always dead-on, which could blur things slightly.
  • A handful of the earliest readings recorded direction but not exact size, so they count toward “how often” but not “how far.”

Bottom line

On my guitar, the old advice held up: finishing a string by tuning up to pitch really did keep it in tune better — out of tune about 16 percentage points less often, and drifting a little less far. The effect is modest, not dramatic, and it’s one instrument’s worth of evidence. But if you’ve ever wondered whether “always tune up” is worth the small extra fuss of overshooting and coming back, this experiment says: yes, probably.
